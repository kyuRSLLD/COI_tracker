Additional Insured vs. Certificate Holder: What Remodelers and GCs Actually Need

November 18, 20266 min read

Ask ten contractors what the certificate holder box does, and most will say it means the sub’s insurance covers them.

It does not. Certificate holder status gives you one thing: proof that a certificate was issued to you. Coverage — a defense and indemnity when the sub’s work causes a loss — comes from being an additional insured, and that requires the policy itself or an endorsement.

The difference between additional insured and certificate holder is something nobody thinks about until a claim. Then it is the whole case.

The difference in one sentence

A certificate holder receives evidence of insurance. An additional insured receives coverage under the policy.

Certificate holder Additional insured
What it is The name in the box at the bottom of the ACORD 25 An entity added to the sub’s policy by endorsement
What you get Proof a certificate was issued to you — useful at audit and to owners Defense and indemnity for claims arising from the sub’s work
How it happens The broker types your name into the form CG 20 10, CG 20 37, or an equivalent endorsement
What it does not do Grant coverage, rights, or guaranteed notice Survive a policy that excludes your project type

Two myths that cost money

Myth 1 — “The description of operations says we’re covered.” Brokers often type helpful-sounding language into that box — “ABC General Contracting is additional insured for ongoing operations.” Typing it does not make it so. The certificate’s own disclaimer says it does not amend, extend, or alter coverage. Coverage exists if, and only if, the endorsement exists in the policy.

Myth 2 — “The ADDL INSD box is checked Y, so we’re set.” A checked box means the broker believes an endorsement applies. It is a prompt to verify, not a confirmation. The endorsement may cover ongoing work only, exclude your project type, require a written contract you never signed, or never have been issued at all.

CG 20 10 vs. CG 20 37

Two ISO forms carry most of the weight in construction.

Form Covers Example
CG 20 10 Ongoing operations A sub’s employee trips over their own extension cord while the job is in progress.
CG 20 37 Completed operations A soldered joint fails inside a wall six months after the remodel.

The most common — and most expensive — gap in construction is a sub who has CG 20 10 but not CG 20 37. The certificate looks clean and the box is checked, but completed operations is missing. For electrical, plumbing, roofing, framing, and siding, an ongoing-operations-only endorsement is close to worthless: the claims that hurt arrive after the crew is gone.

The other two requirements

Waiver of subrogation (CG 24 04 or equivalent). When the sub’s insurer pays a claim, subrogation lets it step into the sub’s shoes and sue whoever contributed to the loss — usually you. A waiver gives up that right. It must apply to workers’ comp as well as general liability; a GL-only waiver leaves the WC carrier free to come after you.

Primary and non-contributory. Primary means the sub’s policy pays first. Non-contributory means its insurer cannot seek a contribution split from yours. Without that wording, two carriers argue over the loss, your policy gets drawn in, and your loss history pays for it at renewal.

The clause to put in every subcontract

“Subcontractor shall name [Your Company] as an Additional Insured on a primary and non-contributory basis for both ongoing operations (ISO CG 20 10 or equivalent) and completed operations (ISO CG 20 37 or equivalent) under its Commercial General Liability policy, and shall provide a Waiver of Subrogation (ISO CG 24 04 or equivalent) in favor of [Your Company] on all applicable policies, including Workers’ Compensation.”

Three traps for remodelers and home repair

1. A blanket endorsement still needs a written contract. Many subs carry blanket additional insured forms that extend coverage to anyone the sub is required to insure by written contract. Hire them by text and there is no written contract, so the blanket never activates. A one-page agreement with a proper insurance clause is the minimum.

2. Exclusions can gut a valid endorsement. A continuous-damage exclusion can eliminate completed-work property damage. An open-roof exclusion does the same for roofers. A residential exclusion bites a commercial sub taking side work in a house. Read the endorsement together with the exclusions, not instead of them.

3. Homeowners are asking for proof. Clients increasingly want to see that everyone working in their home is insured. A GC who can send a clean, shareable compliance record turns insurance from a cost center into a reason to be hired. That is what remodeling contractor compliance looks like from the client’s side.

Endorsement verification checklist

# Check Why it matters
1 Named insured matches the legal entity on your subcontract Coverage attaches to the named insured
2 CG 20 10 and CG 20 37 both present Ongoing-only leaves post-job defect claims on you
3 You are named specifically or by valid blanket wording Blankets require a written contract
4 Primary and non-contributory wording confirmed Stops your policy from contributing
5 Waiver of subrogation covers GL and WC Insurers cannot recover from you after paying
6 Policy numbers and dates match the certificate Mismatched forms may be stale or for another policy
7 Issued forms, not specimen pages Brokers sometimes attach samples
8 Expiration date tracked for renewal The whole package lapses on the policy date

FAQ

Does being the certificate holder make me an additional insured?

No. The certificate holder is the party receiving proof of insurance. Additional insured status requires the policy or an endorsement naming you. The certificate itself states that it confers no rights on the holder.

Is CG 20 37 required if the sub already has CG 20 10?

Not by law — but commercially, for any trade whose defects show up later, yes. Put both forms in the subcontract by name and verify both are attached. An ongoing-operations-only endorsement covers the construction period and nothing after it.

Can software verify that I am covered?

No, and any tool that says so is overselling. Software can read the certificate and endorsement pages, compare them to your requirements, and flag gaps or mismatches. A person decides whether the file is acceptable, and the insurer decides claims. What software does well is make sure nothing sits unread in a folder.
