How General Contractors Pass a Workers' Comp Audit With Subcontractors

November 18, 20266 min read

Your carrier prices your policy on estimated payroll. Once a year, the workers’ comp audit compares that estimate to what you actually paid — and for a general contractor, the gap is almost always subcontractors.

If a sub can’t be proven to carry their own workers’ comp, the auditor adds what you paid them to your payroll and bills you at your rates. One undocumented crew can produce a five-figure invoice. The paperwork that prevents it takes about ten minutes per sub.

The rule behind every audit

Two things make contract labor premium-bearing.

State law. If an uninsured sub is injured on your site, most states can treat them as your employee for workers’ comp purposes. The system wants the injured worker covered; if the sub didn’t carry their own coverage, responsibility rolls uphill to the GC who hired them.

Carrier exposure. The carrier might have had to pay that claim, so it charges premium for the risk whether or not a claim happened.

The burden of proof is yours. “They’re a 1099” is a tax filing, not a coverage determination. “They said they were insured” is a phone call. Proof is a certificate issued to your company, with policy dates that cover the days the sub actually worked.

Field rule: If you can’t produce a valid COI for every day a sub was on site, budget to pay for that sub twice.

Three traps that produce the surprise bill

1. The gap-year sub. Certificate on file in January, expired in April, paid in May. The auditor includes every dollar paid after the lapse date. Most audit bills are born here — not with subs who never had coverage, but with subs whose coverage quietly ended mid-job.

2. The blended invoice. A sub bills “$18,000, materials and labor” with no split. Many auditors treat the whole invoice as labor exposure and apply your rate to all of it. Require labor and materials on separate lines; it is the cheapest audit protection you can buy.

3. The vague scope. If the file doesn’t show what trade the sub performed, payroll can land in a higher-rated class. Roofing and painting are not priced the same. Contracts and scopes of work are what keep the class code honest.

The six-document sub packet

No packet, no job. Before any sub — plumber, roofer, framer, handyman — touches a site:

  • Workers’ comp certificate (ACORD 25). Named insured matches the sub’s legal name exactly. Policy dates cover every work day. Check owner and officer exclusions if the owner swings a hammer.
  • General liability certificate. Not a workers’ comp document, but your contract and your client require it. Run the ACORD 25 checklist on this one.
  • W-9. Legal name, EIN, entity type. If the W-9 and the named insured disagree, the file is not clean.
  • Written subcontract. Even two pages. It establishes an independent business and carries the insurance clause. Draft one with the free insurance clause generator.
  • Invoices that split labor and materials. Make it a contract term, not a favor.
  • Proof of payment. Check or ACH tied to a specific invoice. Cash is audit poison — no trail means the auditor decides what the payment was for.

The quarterly routine

Audits go badly when they are an annual emergency. They go fine when they are a habit.

When What to do
Onboarding Six-document packet complete. No packet, no start date.
30 days before expiration Renewal request to the sub and the broker. Escalate at day 10.
Quarterly Reconcile the sub list against the check register. Every payment needs coverage on that date.
30 days before policy renewal Export the auditor packet: sub list, certificates, contracts, totals paid per sub.
Audit day Hand over one organized file. Answer from records, never from memory.

Keep a running master list all year: legal name, trade, states worked, total paid, COI status, expiration date. Subcontractor COI tracking software does this without a spreadsheet, and keeps the review decision attached to each certificate.

If the bill already landed

  1. Request the worksheets. You want every sub included and the dollars assigned to each.
  2. Cross-reference payment dates against COI files. The surprise is usually a lapse, not a sub who never had coverage.
  3. Ask brokers for retroactive certificates showing coverage in force on the work dates. Auditors often accept proof that coverage existed, even if the paper arrived late.
  4. Challenge blended invoices and class codes with invoice splits and scopes of work.
  5. Put it in writing and route it through your insurance broker. Most carriers allow a dispute window — ask what yours is, in days.

FAQ

Does a 1099 subcontractor need workers’ comp?

In most states, yes — their own policy or yours. A 1099-NEC changes how you report the payment, not who carries the exposure. If an uninsured sub is injured, the claim can land on your policy and your experience modifier.

What happens if the sub’s policy expires mid-job?

The days after expiration are the exposure. Require a renewal certificate before the expiration date and hold new scheduling until it arrives. “Active today” is not a compliance status; coverage through the last day of work is.

Is the certificate enough, or do I need the policy?

For audit purposes, a valid certificate usually satisfies the auditor. For claims, the endorsements and policy forms decide — and the certificate itself says it grants no coverage. Get both for high-exposure trades. Endorsement verification is where that file gets built.

The bottom line

Passing an audit is not about your relationship with your insurance rep. It is documentation discipline: a complete packet at onboarding, expiration dates tracked, invoices split, and a quarterly reconciliation before the auditor finds the gap.
