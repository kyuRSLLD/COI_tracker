Waiver of Subrogation: What General Contractors Need From Every Sub

November 18, 20266 min read

The sub’s general liability carrier pays a homeowner $180,000 after a roof leak. Six months later the same carrier writes to your company demanding reimbursement, arguing that your scheduling and site protection contributed to the loss.

That is subrogation: the carrier steps into the sub’s shoes and comes after you. A waiver of subrogation is what stops it. Most GCs believe they have one because the subcontract says so. Many don’t.

What subrogation actually is

When an insurer pays a claim, it acquires the insured’s right to recover from whoever caused the loss. In construction, that is often the GC — for coordination, sequencing, temporary protection, or site conditions.

The sequence is predictable. The sub’s carrier pays, the carrier sues you, your carrier defends you, and your loss history absorbs the claim whether you win or lose.

Two waivers, not one

General liability. The waiver appears as an endorsement to the sub’s GL policy — ISO CG 24 04 or the carrier’s equivalent. It gives up the carrier’s right to recover from you for a covered loss.

Workers’ compensation. This is a separate endorsement on the WC policy, and it is the one GCs forget. Without it, the sub’s WC carrier can pursue you for benefits paid to an injured worker — including on a claim your own WC policy is already handling.

The GL waiver is the one everybody asks for. The WC waiver is the one that produces the ugly letter.

The subcontract clause is not the waiver

This is the most expensive misunderstanding in risk transfer.

A waiver clause in your subcontract is a promise between you and the sub. The carrier never signed it. Unless the policy permits the insured to waive subrogation before a loss, or an endorsement grants the waiver, the carrier can still pursue you.

Your protection is the endorsement page — not the contract clause, and not the SUBR WVD checkbox on the certificate. That checkbox says a waiver may exist. It does not prove one was issued, and it says nothing about workers’ comp.

Blanket wording gives up subrogation against all entities the insured is required by written contract to waive — which is why handshake hiring undoes it. Scheduled wording names you, sometimes project by project: narrower, and easier to verify.

What to check on the endorsement

  • GL waiver endorsement page (CG 24 04 or equivalent)
  • Separate workers’ comp waiver endorsement
  • Your legal name, or blanket wording tied to a written contract
  • Written subcontract that requires the waiver
  • Policy numbers and dates match the certificate
  • Waiver reaches completed operations where the form allows
  • Expiration tracked with the rest of the package

Run this at onboarding and at every renewal. A waiver that expired in March is not a waiver in June, which is why subcontractor COI tracking treats expiration as a compliance event rather than an administrative one.

Waiver vs. additional insured

These are different tools and GCs routinely confuse them.

Protection What it does What it does not do
Additional insured Gives you defense and indemnity under the sub’s policy Stop the sub’s carrier from pursuing you for a loss it paid
Waiver of subrogation Stops the carrier from recovering from you after paying Give you coverage or a defense

You need both. Additional insured status handles the claim; the waiver handles the recovery that follows it.

Where the waiver gets tested

Two places, mostly.

A completed-operations loss. The sub’s work fails two years later, the carrier pays the homeowner, and it looks for a party to reimburse it. Your waiver is the first document it reads.

A workers’ comp claim. An employee of the sub is injured on your site. The WC carrier pays benefits, then examines whether your site or supervision contributed. A GL-only waiver does nothing here.

Residential remodelers meet both more often than commercial GCs: occupied homes, water damage, and homeowners who call their own carrier first. The residential compliance requirements are stricter for that reason.

Put it in the contract

The clause should name the waiver, both policies, and the endorsement:

“Subcontractor shall provide a Waiver of Subrogation in favor of [Your Company] on its Commercial General Liability and Workers’ Compensation policies, with endorsements attached to the certificate.”

FAQ

Is a waiver of subrogation the same as additional insured?

No. Additional insured gives you coverage and a defense under the sub’s policy. A waiver gives up the carrier’s right to recover from you after it pays. They solve different problems, and you want both.

Does the waiver have to be in the subcontract?

The subcontract creates the obligation to provide it. The endorsement is what binds the carrier. Ask for the endorsement page, not the promise.

Can a carrier refuse to honor a waiver?

If the waiver was granted without policy authority or an endorsement, yes. That is why the endorsement page matters more than the clause, and why the checkbox on the certificate is not evidence.

The bottom line

Subrogation is how a claim you thought was closed comes back. The defense is a waiver the carrier actually agreed to — on GL and on workers’ comp — collected before work starts and renewed with the policy.
