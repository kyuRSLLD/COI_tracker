What Is a COI? A General Contractor's Guide to Subcontractor Insurance Certificates

November 25, 20266 min read

What is a COI? It is one page: these carriers, these policy numbers, these limits, in force the day the form printed. It is the first document you collect from a sub.

A COI tells you a policy existed. It does not tell you the policy covers your job, your loss, or the work the sub is about to do — and that gap is where losses land.

The short answer

A certificate of insurance (COI) lists a subcontractor’s carriers, policy numbers, coverage types, limits, and effective and expiration dates. In the US it almost always arrives on a standard form called the ACORD 25, prepared by the sub’s agent, usually free and often the same day.

You appear on it as the certificate holder. That means you were sent a copy. It does not make you a party to the policy and it grants no coverage.

Why general contractors collect them

Insurance follows the work. When an uninsured sub causes a loss, the cost travels up the contract chain to whoever signed for the job.

Three routes worth knowing by name:

  • A claim on the job. A sub damages property or injures someone with no coverage, and the owner looks to the GC’s carrier.
  • An injured worker. In many states a GC can be held responsible for workers’ comp benefits owed to an uninsured sub’s employees.
  • Your premium audit. Subs without proof of coverage get treated as your payroll, and you are billed for them.

Most COI requirements come from contracts, not statutes — owners require them of GCs, and GCs pass the terms down. Confirm the details with your agent.

How to read an ACORD 25

Read top to bottom and check each section against the subcontract, not what the sub said on the phone.

Section What it shows What to check
Producer The agent who issued the certificate A real agency with a phone number you can call
Insured Legal name and address of the policyholder An exact match to the entity that signed your subcontract
Insurers and policy numbers Carriers lettered A through F with NAIC numbers, one policy per coverage line Every coverage line ties to a letter above it
Coverages General liability, auto, umbrella or excess, workers’ comp, employers’ liability Every coverage your contract requires is present
Dates Effective and expiration date per policy The span covers your whole project
Limits Per occurrence, aggregate, per accident Each limit meets your contract minimum
ADDL INSD and SUBR WVD Columns marked when additional insured status or a waiver applies Marked where required, and backed by the endorsement itself
Description and holder Free text for project names; who the certificate was issued to Your project named where required, your legal name spelled correctly

What to require from your subcontractors

Your subcontract sets the requirement and the certificate is how you check it. These figures are common starting points, not rules.

Coverage What it pays for Commonly requested limit
Commercial general liability Property damage and bodily injury from the sub’s work $1M per occurrence, $2M aggregate
Commercial auto Accidents involving the sub’s vehicles $1M combined single limit
Workers’ compensation Medical costs and lost wages for injured employees Statutory limits for the state
Umbrella or excess Limits above the policies listed here $1M to $5M, scaled to trade and job size

Beyond limits, most GCs ask for four policy terms: additional insured status for the GC and often the owner, covering ongoing and completed operations; primary and non-contributory wording, so the sub’s policy responds first; a waiver of subrogation, so the sub’s carrier cannot pursue you for what it paid; and notice of cancellation, where the carrier will endorse it.

Every one of those lives in an endorsement, not on the certificate. Ask for the endorsement pages with the certificate and read the form numbers — what gets checked and why walks through the common ones.

What a certificate cannot tell you

A COI is evidence that a policy existed on one date. The form says so in its own disclaimer. Keep four limits in mind.

  1. It goes stale. A sub can miss a premium payment the week after the certificate issues. The policy cancels and nobody has to tell the certificate holder.
  2. It does not change the policy. A checked additional insured box means nothing if the endorsement was never added.
  3. It is silent on exclusions. Policies sold to small contractors often exclude roofing, residential work, work above a set height, or injuries to the sub’s own crews. None of that prints on the form.
  4. It can be edited. Certificates travel as PDFs, and changing a date or a limit takes minutes.

None of this makes the certificate worthless. It starts the review.

A five-minute review before the sub starts work

Run every certificate through the same list before the sub mobilizes.

  • The certificate came from the agent or broker, not forwarded by the sub
  • The insured name matches the subcontract exactly, including LLC or Inc.
  • Every required coverage appears, with limits at or above your minimums
  • No policy expires before the sub’s work is scheduled to finish
  • Additional insured and waiver boxes are marked, and the endorsement pages are attached
  • Your company is named correctly as certificate holder

A few things should slow you down: fonts or alignment that change partway through the form, limits that look unusually round for a small sub, a producer you cannot reach, and a workers’ comp line left blank by a sub with a crew. When something looks off, call the agency and ask.

Remodelers have a second problem: residential exclusions are common on policies sold to subs who mostly do commercial work. What remodeling contractors run into covers the forms to ask for.

Keeping certificates current

Collecting one certificate is the easy part. Keeping every sub’s file current is the work.

Policies renew on their own calendars. A GC with 30 subs carrying three or four policies each is tracking roughly 100 expiration dates a year. In a spreadsheet and an inbox, some get missed, and the missed one surfaces after a claim or at the audit.

Subcontractor COI tracking is the part that has to run without a Monday morning: collect the certificates, read the limits and endorsement columns, and see who is covered before they hit the schedule. Frequently asked questions

Who issues a COI?

The subcontractor’s insurance agent or broker issues it on behalf of the carriers. The sub requests it and names you as certificate holder.

How long is a COI good for?

A certificate has no life of its own. It is useful until the earliest policy expiration date printed on it, and only if the policy stays in force until then.

Is a certificate holder the same as an additional insured?

No. A certificate holder receives a copy. An additional insured is covered under the policy by endorsement and can make a claim on it.

What if a sub has no employees and no workers’ comp?

State rules for sole proprietors vary. Ask your agent what your own carrier accepts at audit, which is often a state exemption certificate or a signed waiver.
